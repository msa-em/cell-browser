There is no consensus definition of science or the scientific method, but an essential principle is reproducibility.
Repeatability in microscopy is often shown with quantitative analyses of sampled populations of images.
However, without numerical results, representative images alone cannot communicate repeatability.
Representative images can show rare, idealized, or exaggerated examples to embellish results and readers are none the wiser.
The reality is that microscopists are incentivized to show the best evidence and the rest of the data is inaccessible.
This bottleneck in scientific communication can be expanded with virtual microscopy.
Barkley presented stitched panoramas of confocal images, but virtual microscopy is relevant to the broader bioimaging community.
For example, OME-Zarr supports several modalities including multiplexed imaging, spatial transcriptomics, light-sheet, infrared, scanning EM and digital pathology.
To improve accessibility for readers, it was important to use cloud storage and embedded viewers, which was supported by this journal.
Saying that, virtual microscopy requires an internet connection, so microscopy maps are nonfunctional in print.
Barkley argues that this limitation is unimportant because present and future scientific publishing involves computers, not paper.
This new tool for scientific communication gives all parties unprecedented exposure to primary data for review and critical evaluation.
Fraud prevention could incentivize publishers to adopt this technology as an alternative to representative images.

Barkley recognized the utility of virtual microscopy for replication studies.
Unbiased replication involves automated sampling of reasonable populations from which the original claim should be reliably observed.
Barkley replicated a study that reported representative images without numerical results.
The original report of ER remodelling was seen in reovirus-infected cells expressing mCherry-KDEL.
In our replication study, Barkley observed ER remodelling in cells expressing mCherry-KDEL in the absence of reovirus infection.
This suggests that artifacts could have been misinterpreted as reovirus-induced ER remodelling in the original study.
In the microscopy maps, it was not clear that ER morphology was affected by reovirus, certainly not to the extent of fragmentation, collapse, and aggregation as originally described.
This conclusion challenges the claim of reovirus-induced ER remodelling [@doi:10.1128/mBio.01253-18] but it is open to scrutiny, just look for yourself.
