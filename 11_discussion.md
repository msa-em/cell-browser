There is no consensus definition of science or the scientific method, but an essential principle is reproducibility.
Repeatability in microscopy is often shown with quantitative analyses of the sampled population of images.
However, without numerical results, representative images alone cannot communicate repeatability.
In fact, representative images can show rare, exaggerated, or idealized examples to embellish results while readers are none the wiser.
The concern is that microscopists are incentivized to show the best evidence with pretty pictures, while the rest of the dataset is inaccessible to readers.
Virtual microscopy is an alternative visual communication tool that transmits images over the internet.
Barkley used stitched panoramas of confocal microscopy data, but virtual micrscopy is applicable for the broader bioimaging community.
For example, OME-Zarr supports several modalities including multiplexed imaging, spatial transcriptomics, light-sheet, infrared, scanning EM and digital pathology.
To lower accessibility barriers to review the data, it was important to use cloud storage and embedded viewers, which was uniquely supported by the Elemental Microscopy journal.
Saying that, the microscopy maps require an internet connection so will be nonfunctional in print.
Barkley considers this limitation unimportant because current and future scientific discourse involves computers, not paper.
This new tool for scientific communication gives all parties unprecedented data exposure for review and critical evaluation.
Fraud prevention could incentivize publishers to adopt this technology in journals as an alternative to representative images.

Barkley recognized the utility of virtual microscopy for replication studies.
Unbiased replication involves automated sampling of reasonable populations from which the original claim should be reliably observed.
Barkley replicated a study that reported representative images without numerical results.
The original report of ER remodelling was seen in reovirus-infected cells expressing mCherry-KDEL.
In our replication study, Barkley observed ER remodelling in cells expressing mCherry-KDEL in the absence of reovirus infection.
This suggests that artifacts could have been misinterpreted as reovirus-induced ER remodelling in the original study.
In the microscopy maps, it was not clear that ER morphology was affected by reovirus, certainly not to the extent of fragmentation, collapse, and aggregation as originally described.
This conclusion challenges the claim of reovirus induced ER-remodelling [@doi:10.1128/mBio.01253-18] and it is open to scrutiny, just look for yourself.
