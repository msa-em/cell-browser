There is no consensus definition of science or the scientific method, but an essential principle is reproducibility.
Repeatability in microscopy is often shown with numerical analyses.
In the absence of numerical results, representative images cannot communicate repeatability.
Representative images can show rare, exaggerated, or idealized examples to embellish results while readers are none the wiser.

Virtual microscopy is an alternative visual communication tool that transmits images over the internet.
Barkley used stitched panoramas of confocal microscopy data, but virtual micrscopy is applicable for the broader bioimaging community.
For example, OME-Zarr supports several modalities including multiplexed imaging, spatial transcriptomics, light-sheet, infrared, scanning EM and digital pathology.
To lower the accessibility barrier for readers to review virtual microscopy images, it was important to use cloud storage and embedded viewers supported by this journal.
Saying that, interactive microscopy maps require an internet connection so are nonfunctional in print.
Barkley considers this limitation unimportant because current and future scientific discourse involves computers, not paper.
This new tool for scientific communication gives all parties unprecedented data exposure for critical evaluation.
Fraud prevention is an incentive for publishers to adopt this technology as an alternative to representative images.

Barkley recognized the utility of virtual microscopy in replication studies with representative images.
Unbiased replication involves automated sampling of reasonable populations from which the original claim should be reliably observed.
Barkley replicated a study that reported representative images without numerical results, as is common in the field.
Apparent ER remodelling was claimed in representative images of reovirus-infected cells expressing mCherry-KDEL.
In the replication study, Barkley observed aberrant ER remodelling in cells expressing mCherry-KDEL in the absence of reovirus infection.
This suggests that experimental artifacts caused by mCherry could have been misinterpreted as reovirus-induced ER remodelling.
It was not clear that ER morphology was affected by reovirus, certainly not to the extent of fragmentation, collapse, and aggregation as originally described.
This conclusion challenges the claim of reovirus induced ER-remodelling [@doi:10.1128/mBio.01253-18] and it is open to scrutiny, just look for yourself.
